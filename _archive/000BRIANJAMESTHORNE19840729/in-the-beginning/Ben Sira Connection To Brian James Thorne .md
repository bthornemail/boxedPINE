## 🧩 1. **Who was Ben Sira?**

**Ben Sira** (בן סירא) means **“son of Sira”** in Hebrew or Aramaic.
His full name in Greek is rendered:

> Ἰησοῦς υἱὸς Σειράχ — *Jesus son of Sirach*
> (in Hebrew/Aramaic: **Yeshua ben Sira**)

He lived in **Jerusalem** around **180–175 B.C.E.**, during the Second Temple period.
He was a **Jewish scribe and wisdom teacher**, writing in Hebrew (possibly with later edits in Greek). His work is known by several names:

* **“The Wisdom of Ben Sira”**
* **“The Wisdom of Jesus son of Sirach”**
* **“Ecclesiasticus”** (“church book” — the Latin name used by early Christians)

---

## 📜 2. **The Text Itself**

Ben Sira’s book is part of the **Wisdom literature** tradition, along with **Proverbs**, **Job**, and **Ecclesiastes**. It focuses on:

* Ethics, piety, and social behavior,
* Reverence for Torah,
* Wisdom as divine illumination,
* Practical moral philosophy.

It was originally **written in Hebrew**, translated into **Greek** by Ben Sira’s **grandson**, who added a prologue explaining his translation process (around 132 B.C.E.).
That prologue is one of the earliest explicit discussions of **translation theory** in ancient literature.

---

## 📖 3. **Relation to the Bible**

| Canon                                | Status of *Ben Sira*                                   |
| ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Jewish Tanakh**                    | ❌ Not included (non-canonical)                         |
| **Catholic Bible**                   | ✅ Included (as *Ecclesiasticus*)                       |
| **Eastern Orthodox Bible**           | ✅ Included                                             |
| **Protestant Bible**                 | ❌ Usually excluded (considered *Apocrypha*)            |
| **Septuagint (Greek Old Testament)** | ✅ Included — hence its influence on early Christianity |

Even though it’s outside the Jewish canon, **Ben Sira was highly respected by rabbis**.
He’s quoted several times in the **Talmud** and **Midrash** (e.g., *Hagigah 13a*, *Baba Kamma 92b*), sometimes anonymously as “the author of a wise saying.”

---

## ✝️ 4. **Connection to Jesus and the New Testament**

While *Ben Sira* was written about **two centuries before Jesus of Nazareth**, it had a profound **conceptual influence** on early Christian thought.

**a. Shared themes:**

* The identification of **Wisdom (Sophia)** with **God’s Word (Logos)** parallels how the Gospel of John later presents Jesus as the Logos (“Word made flesh”).
* Both emphasize humility, righteousness, and divine wisdom revealed in daily life.
* *Sirach* 24 personifies Wisdom in a way that early Christians later reinterpreted **Christologically**.

**b. Name coincidence:**
The author’s Hebrew name **Yeshua** (rendered “Jesus”) is the same as that of **Jesus of Nazareth** — though they’re completely different people.
This linguistic overlap sometimes confused early readers, but it also symbolically linked *Ben Sira*’s wisdom to **Christ as divine wisdom** in early Christian typology.

**c. Canonical echoes:**
Many New Testament verses echo Sirach’s ethical tone — e.g.:

* *Sirach 28:2* → “Forgive your neighbor’s wrong, then when you pray, your sins will be forgiven.”
  ↔ *Matthew 6:14* — “If you forgive others, your heavenly Father will forgive you.”
* *Sirach 3:17–18* — “My child, conduct yourself with humility…”
  ↔ *Luke 14:11* — “Whoever exalts himself will be humbled…”

---

## 🕎 5. **Name and Symbolism (“Thorn”)**

You mentioned earlier that *Sira* might mean “thorn.”
That’s an interesting — though uncertain — etymological note. If it does derive from **Aramaic *sira* = “thorn”**, the name *Ben Sira* (“son of the thorn”) could have had a figurative connotation like:

* “One who pierces or pricks the conscience,”
* “A sharp one” (wise, discerning), or
* “One who is guarded or protected (by thorns).”

Such wordplay would fit his personality as a moral teacher whose words could “sting” but heal — a common image in prophetic and wisdom literature.
